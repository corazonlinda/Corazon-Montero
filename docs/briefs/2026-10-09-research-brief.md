1. Who sets the assurance requirement, and what is it today?
The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers has the authority to require financial assurance, such as a bond, for the Dakota Access Pipeline. Although the company is responsible for applicable cleanup costs and damages caused by an oil spill, the available documents do not disclose how much insurance or financial assurance DAPL currently maintains. Federal regulations previously established a liability limit of approximately $725.7 million for onshore facilities, including pipelines. However, this is different from the amount of insurance or bond coverage DAPL actually maintains. The current liability limit and whether it applies to DAPL still need to be confirmed. Federal law also allows liability to exceed this limit under certain circumstances, including gross negligence or willful misconduct. [Rows 10, 12, 17, 19]
2. What expected loss does the EIS probability imply, and over what period?
According to the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS), the estimated return period for an oil release of any size, based on historical pipeline data, is approximately 744 years. After accounting for the pipeline's design and the conditions considered in the worst-case discharge analysis, the EIS provides a longer return period of approximately 37,207 years. However, the EIS estimates that a release exceeding 10,000 barrels under the same adjusted analysis would occur much less frequently, approximately once every 5,774,148 years. These estimates represent different categories of oil releases and should not be treated as the same probability.
Using the EIS's worst-case discharge estimate of 16,996 barrels at Lake Oahe and the reported costs of two previous oil spills, I estimated that the potential damages could range from approximately $934.78 million to $2.29 billion. These are my own estimates based on the costs of previous spills, rather than damages specifically calculated by the EIS for Lake Oahe. Although the probability of a major spill is extremely low, I believe it is still important to consider how much damage could occur if one actually happened. [Rows 1, 3, 6, 21, 22, 23]
Estimated spill damages:
16,996 barrels × $55,000 per barrel (Kalamazoo spill comparison) = $934,780,000
16,996 barrels × $135,000 per barrel (Yellowstone spill comparison) = $2,294,460,000
Calculation A: Assuming every release in the design-adjusted worst-case discharge analysis resulted in these damages
$934,780,000 ÷ 37,207 years = approximately $25,124 per year
$2,294,460,000 ÷ 37,207 years = approximately $61,667 per year
These calculations assume that every release in this category would cause damages equivalent to a major spill. This is unlikely because many releases would be smaller and would not necessarily result in the same costs.
Calculation B: Using the historical probability of a release exceeding 10,000 barrels
$934,780,000 ÷ 115,483 years = approximately $8,095 per year
$2,294,460,000 ÷ 115,483 years = approximately $19,868 per year
Calculation C: Using the probability of a release exceeding 10,000 barrels after accounting for the pipeline's design
$934,780,000 ÷ 5,774,148 years = approximately $162 per year
$2,294,460,000 ÷ 5,774,148 years = approximately $397 per year
The last calculation is a better comparison for my analysis because the estimated 16,996-barrel spill falls within the category of releases exceeding 10,000 barrels. However, this does not mean that every release exceeding 10,000 barrels would cause the same amount of damage or that the oil would necessarily reach Lake Oahe.
Time period: These calculations estimate the expected financial loss over one year based on the probabilities and damages I used. If I use a ten-year period and assume that the probability and estimated damages remain the same, the expected loss using the design-adjusted probability would be approximately $1,620 to $3,970. I am using ten years as an example rather than assuming that this is the pipeline's remaining operating life.
3. At what damage figure does the assurance stop covering the expected loss, and what evidence says whether a spill could cost that much?
Since the available documents do not disclose DAPL's actual financial assurance amount, I am using the previously identified $725.7 million federal liability limit as a comparison.
Based on the estimated costs from the Kalamazoo and Yellowstone oil spills, a spill between approximately 5,400 and 13,200 barrels could result in damages exceeding this amount. The EIS estimates a worst-case discharge of 16,996 barrels at Lake Oahe, which is greater than both figures. For comparison, the Kalamazoo spill cost approximately $1.039 billion, while the Yellowstone spill cost approximately $135 million. Although neither spill occurred in a drinking-water lake, these examples show how expensive an oil spill can become and why the amount of financial assurance available is important. [Rows 6, 17, 18, 21, 22]
Calculations:
$725,700,000 ÷ $55,000 per barrel (Kalamazoo cost estimate) = approximately 13,195 barrels
$725,700,000 ÷ $135,000 per barrel (Yellowstone cost estimate) = approximately 5,376 barrels
Estimated comparison range: approximately 5,400–13,200 barrels.
This range shows the estimated spill sizes at which damages could exceed the liability figure used for comparison. It does not show when DAPL's actual insurance or financial assurance would run out.
The expected annual loss of approximately $162 to $397 is very small compared with the $725.7 million figure. However, my calculations show that the greatest financial concern is not the average expected loss, but the possibility of a rare spill that could result in damages exceeding the amount available to cover them.
These calculations also assume that the cost of a spill increases at the same rate as the number of barrels released. However, the actual cost could be different depending on where the spill occurs, how much oil is released, and how difficult it is to clean up. Therefore, I am using the costs of the previous spills as estimates rather than assuming that a spill at Lake Oahe would cost exactly the same amount per barrel.
4. Who bears the cost the assurance does not cover?
The company operating the pipeline is responsible for applicable cleanup costs and damages, but this does not necessarily mean that all expenses would be immediately covered if a major oil spill occurred. For example, if a spill cost $1.039 billion, as reported for Kalamazoo, approximately $313.3 million would exceed the previously identified $725.7 million liability figure. However, this is only a comparison and does not establish how much the company would actually be required to pay.
The Oil Spill Liability Trust Fund could potentially help cover eligible expenses, although not every financial loss is guaranteed to be reimbursed.
The EIS identifies 14 water supply intakes within 160 river miles, including one that provides drinking water to the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. According to the EIS, the tribe's drinking-water intake is approximately 75 miles downstream and 60 to 80 feet deep. Based on the modeled spill scenarios, the intake was not predicted to be affected within the 10-day modeling period. This is an important finding because it suggests that the tribe's drinking-water supply would likely be protected under the conditions studied. However, the EIS also states that the tribe's agricultural water intakes could be affected by a worst-case oil spill. [Rows 8, 9, 24, 25]
If the agricultural intakes were affected, they might need to be shut down during cleanup. This could interrupt water access for farming and livestock, resulting in lost crops, livestock losses, and additional expenses for affected landowners. The EIS states that Dakota Access would be responsible for applicable cleanup costs and reimbursement for lost crops or livestock. Although the probability of a major spill is extremely low, I believe these potential losses should still be considered when determining how much financial assurance the company should maintain. [Rows 25, 26]
The Corps also included an Alternative Water Supply Plan to address possible disruptions to clean water access. To me, this shows why financial assurance matters even when the modeling suggests that the drinking-water intake would not be affected. A plan can help address a water disruption, but it does not establish how much money is available to cover the costs.
Calculation:
$1,039,000,000 (Kalamazoo spill cost) − $725,700,000 (liability figure used for comparison) = $313,300,000
This does not mean that the remaining amount would automatically be paid by taxpayers or the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. Who pays would depend on the company's legal responsibilities, whether the liability limit applies, and which expenses qualify for federal spill funding. [Rows 10, 11, 17, 19, 20, 21, 24, 25, 26]
5. What would you recommend the decision-maker require, and what would tell you a year from now that you were wrong?
I would recommend that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers require DAPL to provide independently verified proof of its current insurance, bond coverage, or other financial security. I believe this information should be provided before determining whether additional coverage is necessary. Based on my calculations, the Corps should consider requiring at least $934.78 million in financial assurance, which is the lower end of my estimated damage range. The final amount could be higher, up to approximately $2.29 billion, depending on the company's existing coverage, the potential damages, and what the Corps is legally allowed to require.
I chose this range based on the estimated costs of two previous oil spills and the EIS's worst-case discharge volume for Lake Oahe. These figures are my own estimates and do not mean that an oil spill at Lake Oahe would necessarily cost this amount.
Some may argue that additional financial assurance is unnecessary because the probability of a major oil spill is extremely low. The EIS also predicts that Standing Rock's drinking-water intake would not be affected under the modeled conditions. I understand these concerns, especially because requiring additional insurance or a larger bond could increase the company's operating expenses. However, the EIS also recognizes that agricultural intakes could be affected, potentially resulting in lost crops, livestock losses, and temporary water disruptions. I believe the company should have enough financial resources to address these potential damages if a spill occurs.
By October 2027, I would reconsider my recommendation if a filing submitted to the Army Corps of Engineers, supported by an independent review, showed that DAPL already maintains enough financial coverage to pay for the estimated damages from a major spill. I would also reconsider the amount if more reliable evidence showed that my calculations significantly overestimated the potential costs of an oil spill at Lake Oahe.
