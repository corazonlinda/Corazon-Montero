# Perfect Competition — engagement brief

## The problem
The farmer decides the optimal mix of mesclun, carrots, and tomatoes to plant and how to divide the available growing space among the beds of crops to maximize profit.

The factors outside of the farmer's control are the types of crops that can be planted, the bed caps, the labor hours required per bed of each crop, the fertilizer cost per bed of each crop, the 64 available beds, the 36-week growing season, the amount of labor available, the fixed cost for the season, diminishing-returns rates, and crop prices determined by the market. Since the farm is a price taker, the farmer has no influence on the market price.

The farmer's limitations are the 64 available beds, the maximum number of beds that can be planted for each crop, and the farm's limited labor. The individual crop caps total 70, but there are only 64 beds available. Therefore, the farmer cannot plant the maximum number of beds for all three crops. Labor requirements increase as additional beds of each crop are planted due to the diminishing-returns rate.

## What I am assuming
I am assuming I will hire all 4 temporary workers and utilize my own labor as well. I am assuming the fertilizer cost differences between crops will not change the crops that I pick. I am assuming the numbers in the case are exactly right with no surprises including the prices, hours and rates. I am assuming that all of the math I calculated was correct. I am assuming that the remaining labor will not be enough to support another tomato bed. I am assuming the lower diminishing rates of mesclun and carrots will ensure they are worth planting to their bed caps. I am assuming that the mix of 30 mesclun beds, 20 carrot beds and 12 tomato beds will be more profitable than any other potential crop mix given the data.

## Hypothesis
I expect the optimal mix to be 30 mesclun beds, 20 carrot beds, and 12 tomato beds because although tomatoes make the most revenue per bed, they require the most labor and have the highest diminishing-returns rate. The revenue per bed is $2,700 for mesclun, $2,094 for carrots, and $8,800 for tomatoes. There are bed availability caps of 30 mesclun beds, 20 carrot beds, and 20 tomato beds. The diminishing-returns rates are 1.25% for mesclun, 2.5% for carrots, and 10% for tomatoes. The labor hours per week per bed are 1.25 hours for mesclun, 0.833 hours for carrots, and 2.5 hours for tomatoes. The diminishing-returns rates and required labor hours are lower for mesclun and carrots, allowing the farm to use more beds instead of exhausting limited labor. The labor requirements for mesclun and carrots increase more slowly as additional beds are planted than they do for tomatoes. Using the labor equation, I calculated that a 30/20/12 mix requires 6,332 labor hours, which is within the 6,480 labor hours available. Increasing the tomato beds from 12 to 13 would increase the total labor to 6,982 hours, putting it over the 6,480 total hours available. I expect the farmer cannot support a 13th tomato bed because the remaining available labor is insufficient. Therefore, I expect the farmer to use 62 of the 64 available beds.

## How I would know I was wrong
If Solver comes back with any other mix than 30 mesclun beds, 20 carrot beds, and 12 tomato beds, I would be wrong. If Solver shows that a 13th tomato bed is feasible, I would be wrong. If Solver finds a different mix that uses the same or less labor but makes more profit than mine, that would be a clear sign my hypothesis was wrong.
