## 2. Economic Model and Calculations

### Calculation 1: Estimated Spill Damages

**Formula:**

Estimated damages = Barrels spilled × Estimated cost per barrel

I will use the costs of the Kalamazoo and Yellowstone oil spills to estimate a possible cost per barrel. Claude suggested this comparison method, and I will explain that it is only an estimate because the circumstances of those spills were different from Lake Oahe. Neither spill happened in a drinking-water lake, and the cost figures were reported by the companies involved. This means the costs are useful for comparison, but they do not tell me exactly what a spill at Lake Oahe would cost.

**Kalamazoo spill:**

- Reported cost: $1,039,000,000 (Row 21)
- Reported volume: Nearly 3 million liters (Row 21)
- Estimated volume after converting liters to barrels: Nearly 3,000,000 L ÷ 158.987 L per barrel ≈ 18,900 barrels

$1,039,000,000 ÷ 18,900 barrels = approximately $55,000 per barrel

**Yellowstone spill:**

- Reported cost: Approximately $135,000,000 (Row 22)
- Reported volume used for this estimate: Approximately 1,000 barrels, confirmed in the Associated Press article (Row 22)

$135,000,000 ÷ 1,000 barrels = $135,000 per barrel

**Lake Oahe worst-case discharge:**

- Estimated worst-case discharge: 16,996 barrels (Row 6)

Using the two estimated costs per barrel:

16,996 × $55,000 = $934,780,000

16,996 × $135,000 = $2,294,460,000

Based on these comparisons, my estimated damage range is approximately $934.78 million to $2.29 billion.

These figures are my own estimates, not official predictions from the EIS. I am assuming that the cost per barrel stays the same as the spill gets larger, even though actual cleanup costs may not increase at a constant rate.

### Calculation 2: Expected Financial Loss

**Formula:**

Expected annual loss = Estimated damages ÷ Return period in years

I plan to use the EIS's design-adjusted return period of 5,774,148 years for a spill exceeding 10,000 barrels. I chose this figure because the EIS's 16,996-barrel worst-case discharge falls within that category.

**Inputs:**

- Estimated damages: $934,780,000 to $2,294,460,000
- Design-adjusted return period: 5,774,148 years (Row 23)

**Calculations:**

$934,780,000 ÷ 5,774,148 = approximately $162 per year

$2,294,460,000 ÷ 5,774,148 = approximately $397 per year

I plan to focus on this calculation in my final paper because it uses the probability category that is most closely related to the size of spill I am studying.

My research brief also included calculations using the 37,207-year return period for releases of all sizes and the historical 115,483-year return period for spills exceeding 10,000 barrels. I may briefly mention these figures to explain why the choice of probability matters, but I do not plan to give all three calculations equal attention in the final paper.

I will explain that my expected loss calculation is simplified. It assumes a spill in the selected category would have damages comparable to my worst-case estimate. It does not mean a spill of that size would necessarily reach Lake Oahe or affect the tribe's water supply.

### Calculation 3: Break-Even Spill Size

**Formula:**

Break-even barrels = Liability figure used for comparison ÷ Estimated cost per barrel

I will use the previously identified $725.7 million federal liability figure to estimate how large a spill would need to be for the estimated damages to exceed that amount.

**Inputs:**

- Federal liability figure used for comparison: $725,700,000 (Row 17)
- Kalamazoo cost estimate: $55,000 per barrel (Row 21)
- Yellowstone cost estimate: $135,000 per barrel (Row 22)

**Calculations:**

$725,700,000 ÷ $55,000 = approximately 13,195 barrels

$725,700,000 ÷ $135,000 = approximately 5,376 barrels

Based on these estimates, a spill of approximately 5,400 to 13,200 barrels could result in damages exceeding the $725.7 million comparison figure.

The EIS's worst-case discharge of 16,996 barrels is larger than both of these amounts.

I will make it clear that the $725.7 million figure is a previously identified federal liability limit, not DAPL's confirmed insurance or financial assurance. If this limit applies to DAPL, the company's liability would generally be limited to that amount unless an exception applies (Row 19). Other eligible costs could potentially be paid through the Oil Spill Liability Trust Fund (Row 20). I still need to establish how those rules apply to DAPL.

### Conditions That Could Change the Outcome

I also want to explain what could happen next under different conditions, rather than assuming that every spill would have the same financial outcome.

- **If a spill is between about 5,400 and 16,996 barrels:** Under at least one of my cost-per-barrel estimates, damages could exceed the $725.7 million comparison figure. The exact crossing point depends on which cost estimate I use.
- **If the federal liability limit applies to DAPL:** The limit could affect how much the company is legally required to pay, unless an exception applies.
- **If DAPL has enough insurance or other financial assurance:** Those resources could help pay covered claims, depending on the terms and applicable law.
- **If DAPL does not have enough financial assurance:** Paying claims could become more difficult or take longer. Some eligible costs might be covered by the OSLTF, but that is not automatic.
- **If agricultural water intakes are affected:** Interrupted water access and damage to crops or livestock could create costs even if the drinking-water intake is not affected under the EIS's modeled conditions.

Because the amount of DAPL's current financial assurance is not stated in the documents I reviewed, I cannot say how much would actually be available after a spill. This is why I want the Corps to independently verify that information before deciding whether additional financial security is needed.
